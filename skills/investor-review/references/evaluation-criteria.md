# Evaluation Criteria Reference

How investors and boards score a business, pitch or initiative. Seven criteria, each with what is really being asked, what "good" looks like by stage, scoring anchors and red flags.

Benchmarks are typical ranges for venture-backed software and tech-enabled businesses (2024–2026). They vary by sector, geography and market cycle. Treat them as the bar an investor will mentally hold, not as laws. When you quote one in a review, label it as a benchmark.

---

## How to Score

### The 1–5 Scale (applies to every criterion)

| Score | Meaning | Investor reaction |
|-------|---------|-------------------|
| **5** | Top decile for this stage. Evidence, not claims. | "This is why we'd lead." |
| **4** | Clearly above the bar for this stage. | "Strong. Not a concern." |
| **3** | At the bar for this stage. Nothing wrong, nothing special. | "Fine. Show me the rest." |
| **2** | Below the bar, or claimed without evidence. Fixable. | "I'd need to see more before committing." |
| **1** | Deal-breaker at this stage, or absent entirely. | "This is why we pass." |

### Scoring Rules
- **Score against the stage, not in absolute terms.** $20K MRR is a 4 at pre-seed and a 1 at Series A.
- **No evidence = 2 at most.** A claim without a number, a customer name, a cohort or a document is not evidence. Write "[not provided]" and cap the score at 2.
- **A single red flag caps the criterion at 2.** A red flag marked **(deal-breaker)** caps it at 1.
- **Half points are not allowed.** Force the call.

### Weighting by Stage

Use the column for the reviewed stage to compute the weighted overall score (sum of score × weight ÷ 100, one decimal).

| Criterion | Bootstrapped | Angel / pre-seed | Seed | Series A | Growth | Corporate |
|-----------|-------------|------------------|------|----------|--------|-----------|
| Market | 15 | 20 | 20 | 15 | 10 | 15 |
| Team | 20 | 35 | 20 | 15 | 10 | 15 |
| Traction | 15 | 5 | 20 | 25 | 25 | 10 |
| Product & moat | 10 | 15 | 15 | 15 | 15 | 15 |
| Why now | 5 | 10 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 |
| Business model | 20 | 10 | 10 | 15 | 15 | 20 |
| Capital efficiency | 15 | 5 | 10 | 10 | 20 | 20 |

**Reading the overall score:**
- **≥ 4.0** — strong; the conversation is about terms, not whether.
- **3.3 – 3.9** — fundable/approvable with work on the weakest criterion.
- **2.5 – 3.2** — not yet; fix the 1s and 2s before pitching.
- **< 2.5** — wrong stage, wrong audience, or wrong business for outside capital right now.

Any criterion scored **1** overrides the average: the verdict cannot be "invest" or "approve" while a 1 stands.

## 1. Market

**What they're really asking:** If this works, how big can it get, and is the founder's sizing honest?

### What Investors Look For
- **Bottom-up sizing**: number of buyers × realistic annual contract value. Top-down "1% of a $50B market" is discounted to zero.
- **Venture math**: can this reach $100M ARR (or a $1B+ outcome) in 7–10 years? A fund needs each lead investment to plausibly return the fund. Example: a $50M seed fund owning 10% at exit needs a ≥ $500M exit to return the fund from one company.
- **Market structure**: growing market, fragmented incumbents, buyers with budget, a clear wedge into a bigger adjacent market.
- **Serviceable reality**: SAM the company can reach with its current channel and price, not the whole industry.

### Good by Stage

| Stage | Good looks like |
|-------|-----------------|
| Bootstrapped | A niche big enough to pay the founders well ($1–10M revenue potential) with buyers reachable without paid acquisition. Venture scale not required. |
| Angel / pre-seed | Credible bottom-up SAM of $500M+; a clear first wedge and a believable story for the expansion market. |
| Seed | Bottom-up SAM validated by early customers' willingness to pay; path to $100M ARR sketched with named adjacent segments. |
| Series A | Sizing confirmed by data: win rates, ACV and penetration in the first segment; second segment showing early pull. |
| Growth | Clear share of a large market; expansion into adjacent products or geographies already producing revenue. |
| Corporate | Market is large enough to move the group's numbers (typically ≥ 1–5% of group revenue within 5 years) and adjacent to existing customers or capabilities. |

### Scoring Anchors
- **5**: Bottom-up SAM ≥ $1B, fast-growing, with evidence the wedge expands.
- **3**: Plausible $300M–$1B SAM, bottom-up, but expansion is a story, not data.
- **1**: Niche under ~$100M with no expansion path (for venture), or sizing that is only top-down.

### Red Flags
- "If we get just 1% of the market…"
- TAM quoted from an analyst report with no bottom-up check
- Buyer has no budget line for this problem
- Market shrinking or consolidating into 1–2 platforms that bundle the feature
- **(deal-breaker for venture)** Honest bottom-up math caps revenue below ~$30M

## 2. Team

**What they're really asking:** Why is this team the one that wins, and will they still be here in five years?

### What Investors Look For
- **Founder-market fit**: lived the problem, sold to this buyer, built this kind of product before, or has unfair access to the channel.
- **Complementary skills**: someone who can build and someone who can sell. Single founders are fundable, but need a clear answer on the gap.
- **Commitment**: full-time founders, meaningful personal stake, standard 4-year vesting with 1-year cliff.
- **Learning speed**: what they believed six months ago that turned out wrong, and what they changed.
- **Ability to hire**: early hires better than the founders in their function.

### Good by Stage

| Stage | Good looks like |
|-------|-----------------|
| Bootstrapped | Founder can sell and deliver alone or with 1–2 people; has a financial runway (savings or side income) of 12+ months. |
| Angel / pre-seed | Founders full-time (or committed date), deep domain insight, shipped a working prototype themselves. |
| Seed | Complete founding team; 1–5 strong early hires; founder still doing sales personally and closing. |
| Series A | Founders proven at hiring; first functional leaders in place or identified (sales, engineering); low regretted attrition. |
| Growth | Executive team that has operated at the next scale; board with independent director; succession thinking. |
| Corporate | Named accountable executive sponsor; a dedicated (not part-time) lead with a track record of launching inside the group. |

### Scoring Anchors
- **5**: Founders who've done this before or have rare, specific insight and access; complete team.
- **3**: Capable, committed team with relevant but not exceptional background.
- **1**: Part-time founders, missing a core skill with no plan, or founder conflict.

### Red Flags
- Founders not full-time at seed or later
- Big skill gap (no technical founder for a deep-tech product; nobody who has sold to the buyer)
- Large "dead equity" on the cap table (departed co-founder holding 20%+)
- Founders own < 50% combined after seed (signals over-dilution or messy history)
- Can't name what they got wrong
- **(deal-breaker)** Integrity issues: misstated metrics, undisclosed litigation, inflated résumés

## 3. Traction

**What they're really asking:** Do customers want this badly enough to pay and stay, and is it accelerating?

### What Investors Look For
- **Revenue quality** over vanity: paying customers, retention and expansion beat sign-ups, waitlists, downloads, followers and impressions.
- **Growth rate and consistency**: month-over-month or year-over-year, with a chart that isn't one spike.
- **Retention**: logo churn, gross revenue retention (GRR), net revenue retention (NRR), cohort curves that flatten.
- **Pull signals**: inbound demand, customers referring others, usage growing without prodding, short sales cycles.

### Good by Stage

| Stage | Good looks like |
|-------|-----------------|
| Bootstrapped | Profitable or default-alive within 6–12 months; revenue covers founders' minimum salaries. |
| Angel / pre-seed | Strong qualitative pull: 10+ design partners, LOIs, a paid pilot, or early revenue ($0–$10K MRR). Usage retention matters more than revenue. |
| Seed | Early repeatable revenue: typically $10K–$100K MRR for B2B SaaS (some seed rounds now expect $500K–$1M ARR), growing 10–20% MoM; consumer: strong D30/D90 retention. |
| Series A | Typically $1–3M+ ARR, growing 2.5–3x YoY; NRR > 100% (SMB) or > 110% (mid-market/enterprise); repeatable channel identified. |
| Growth | $10M+ ARR; growth + profit margin ("Rule of 40") ≥ 40%; NRR 110–130%; predictable pipeline. |
| Corporate | Pilot or market test results with the target customer; for incremental products, attach rate to existing customers. |

### Retention Benchmarks
| Segment | Monthly logo churn | Annual GRR | NRR |
|---------|--------------------|-----------|-----|
| SMB | < 2–3% good | > 80% | > 100% |
| Mid-market | < 1–1.5% | > 85–90% | > 110% |
| Enterprise | < 0.5–1% | > 90–95% | > 120% |

### Scoring Anchors
- **5**: Growth and retention both top-quartile for the stage; cohorts improve over time.
- **3**: Growth at the stage bar; retention acceptable; one channel working.
- **1**: Flat or declining revenue, or high churn masked by new sales.

### Red Flags
- Leading with sign-ups, waitlist, downloads, followers or "pipeline" instead of revenue or retention
- Growth driven by one big customer (> 25–30% of revenue: concentration risk)
- Revenue includes one-off services or pilots presented as ARR
- Cumulative charts used to hide flat monthly numbers
- No cohort data at Series A or later
- **(deal-breaker)** Metrics that don't reconcile with bank statements or contracts

## 4. Product & Moat

**What they're really asking:** Is the product 10x better for someone, and what stops a funded competitor or incumbent from copying it in 12 months?

### What Investors Look For
- **Clear, painful job done much better**: measurable before/after (time saved, revenue gained, cost removed).
- **A defensibility path** (from Hamilton Helmer's *7 Powers*, simplified):
  - **Network effects**: each user makes it more valuable for others
  - **Switching costs**: data, workflows or integrations that are painful to leave
  - **Scale economies**: unit cost falls as volume grows
  - **Proprietary data or technology**: hard-to-replicate data set, patent or research lead
  - **Brand**: trust that commands a price premium
  - **Counter-positioning**: a model incumbents can't copy without hurting their core business
  - **Cornered resource**: exclusive access (license, partnership, talent, distribution)
- **Product velocity**: shipping speed and quality relative to the team size.

### Good by Stage

| Stage | Good looks like |
|-------|-----------------|
| Bootstrapped | Product is clearly better for a niche the big players ignore; moat can be service, speed and relationships. |
| Angel / pre-seed | Working prototype; sharp insight into why existing solutions fail; a credible moat hypothesis. |
| Seed | Product in use weekly by paying customers; moat beginning (integrations, data accumulating, workflow lock-in). |
| Series A | Moat measurable: switching cost shows in retention, network effect in organic acquisition, data advantage in product quality. |
| Growth | Multiple reinforcing moats; platform or multi-product. |
| Corporate | Uses an unfair advantage the group already owns (customers, distribution, data, brand) that a startup can't buy. |

### Scoring Anchors
- **5**: Product clearly 10x for a defined user; at least one moat already measurable.
- **3**: Clearly better product; moat is a credible plan, not yet visible.
- **1**: Feature, not a product; an incumbent or platform could ship it in a quarter.

### Red Flags
- "We have no competitors"
- Moat = "first mover" or "our team executes faster"
- Thin wrapper over a third-party model or API with no proprietary data or workflow
- Core dependency on one platform that can change terms (app store, single marketplace, single API)
- **(deal-breaker)** IP not owned by the company (built by contractors without assignment, or by a founder's employer)

## 5. Why Now

**What they're really asking:** Why wasn't this built five years ago, and why will waiting five more be too late?

### What Investors Look For
A specific change that makes the business possible or urgent:
- **Technology shift**: new capability or a cost curve crossing a threshold (AI models, cheaper sensors, faster networks)
- **Regulatory change**: new mandate, deregulation, compliance deadline
- **Behaviour change**: buyers adopting a new habit or channel
- **Market dislocation**: incumbent stumbling, consolidation, a platform opening up
- **Economic pressure**: labour shortages, cost inflation forcing new solutions

### Good by Stage
| Stage | Good looks like |
|-------|-----------------|
| Bootstrapped | Not required; timing matters only if a window is closing. |
| Angel / pre-seed | A named, dated change with evidence it is real (adoption data, regulation passed). Heavily weighted: timing is most of the bet. |
| Seed / Series A | Change visible in customer behaviour; the company is riding it, not waiting for it. |
| Growth | Tailwind still compounding; risk that it normalises and competition floods in. |
| Corporate | Cost of waiting: share lost, window closing, competitor moves, contract or regulatory deadlines. |

### Scoring Anchors
- **5**: Specific, recent, verifiable shift; the company is clearly positioned on it.
- **3**: Plausible tailwind, generally accepted, not unique to this company.
- **1**: No answer, or the "why now" is years away (too early).

### Red Flags
- "Why now" = "AI" with no specific capability tied to the product
- Depends on a regulation that hasn't passed or a behaviour that hasn't started
- Others have tried and failed with no explanation of what changed

## 6. Business Model

**What they're really asking:** Does each customer make money, and does the price support the sales motion needed to reach scale?

### What Investors Look For
- **Gross margin**: software 70–85%; tech-enabled services 40–60%; marketplaces judged on net revenue (take rate) margin; hardware 30–50%.
- **ARPU / ACV fit with go-to-market motion**:

| Annual contract value (ACV) | Viable sales motion |
|------------------------------|---------------------|
| < $1K | Self-serve, product-led, no human in the loop |
| $1K–$5K | Self-serve with light-touch sales assist |
| $5K–$50K | Inside sales, short cycles |
| $50K–$250K | Field / account executives, 3–6 month cycles |
| > $250K | Enterprise sales, 6–18 month cycles, solution engineering |

  Demo-led sales at a $1K ACV, or field sales at $10K ACV, is a model that won't scale.
- **Venture-scale ARPU**: to reach $100M ARR you need customers × ACV to get there. 10,000 customers at $10K ACV, or 100,000 at $1K. If the reachable customer count × price can't reach $100M, the price or the segment must change.
- **Unit economics**: LTV:CAC ≥ 3:1; CAC payback < 12 months (SMB), < 18 (mid-market), < 24 (enterprise).
- **Expansion levers**: seats, usage, modules, price increases that grow revenue per customer.

### Good by Stage
| Stage | Good looks like |
|-------|-----------------|
| Bootstrapped | Positive contribution margin from customer one; cash collected upfront where possible (annual plans, deposits). |
| Angel / pre-seed | Pricing hypothesis with evidence of willingness to pay; model can reach target gross margin. |
| Seed | Real pricing, gross margin at or near target, early CAC data by channel. |
| Series A | LTV:CAC and CAC payback measured by channel and cohort; expansion revenue appearing. |
| Growth | Efficient at scale; margin expanding; pricing power demonstrated. |
| Corporate | Contribution margin at or above group average; no destructive cannibalisation; pricing consistent with the core brand. |

### Scoring Anchors
- **5**: High gross margin, ACV fits motion, LTV:CAC > 4, expansion revenue proven.
- **3**: Margin at target, unit economics plausible but not yet measured by cohort.
- **1**: Negative gross margin with no path, or a price that can't support the required sales motion.

### Red Flags
- Gross margin < 50% for a "software" company (hidden services or compute costs)
- CAC calculated without salaries, or LTV calculated with optimistic churn
- Pricing set "to win" against a cheaper competitor with no value argument
- Heavy discounting to close deals
- **(deal-breaker for venture)** Reachable customers × realistic price can't reach $50–100M revenue

## 7. Capital Efficiency

**What they're really asking:** How much growth does each dollar buy, and what will this round prove before the money runs out?

### What Investors Look For
- **Burn multiple** = net burn ÷ net new ARR (same period).

| Burn multiple | Rating |
|---------------|--------|
| < 1.0 | Exceptional |
| 1.0 – 1.5 | Great |
| 1.5 – 2.0 | Good |
| 2.0 – 3.0 | Suspect |
| > 3.0 | Bad |

  Early-stage companies often run higher; a seed company below 2.0 stands out.
- **Runway**: 18–24 months after the round closes, to reach the next round's milestones with 6 months of buffer.
- **Use of funds tied to milestones**: "This $2M gets us to $1.2M ARR, NRR 105%, and a repeatable outbound channel by month 18."
- **Dilution**: typically 10–25% per round. More signals a weak position or too-small raise.
- **Default alive** (Paul Graham): at current growth and burn, does the company reach profitability before cash runs out?

### Good by Stage
| Stage | Good looks like |
|-------|-----------------|
| Bootstrapped | Default alive. Every spend has a payback window under 6–12 months. Outside capital optional. |
| Angel / pre-seed | Small raise ($250K–$1.5M typical) that buys 12–18 months and clear proof points. |
| Seed | Raise ($1–4M typical) mapped to Series A milestones; burn multiple trending under 2–3. |
| Series A | Burn multiple < 2; hiring plan tied to revenue capacity (e.g. each AE ramps to quota in 4–6 months). |
| Growth | Burn multiple < 1.5; visible path to profitability; Rule of 40. |
| Corporate | Funding released in stage gates against milestones; payback within the group's policy (often 2–4 years); IRR above hurdle. |

### Scoring Anchors
- **5**: Burn multiple < 1, or profitable while growing; milestones precisely costed.
- **3**: Burn multiple at the stage norm; runway 18 months; use of funds mapped to milestones.
- **1**: < 6 months runway without a bridge plan, or a raise that doesn't reach any fundable milestone.

### Red Flags
- Use of funds = percentages by department with no milestones
- Raise sized to "what investors will give" rather than what the milestones cost
- Hiring ahead of demand (sales team before a repeatable sales process)
- Runway < 6 months at the time of pitching (negotiating from weakness)
- **(deal-breaker)** Raise doesn't cover the cost of reaching the next fundable milestone

## Corporate Initiatives: Reading the Criteria

When the reviewed thing is an internal initiative put to a board or investment committee, read each criterion as follows:

| Criterion | Corporate reading |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Market | Is the opportunity big enough to matter to the group, and adjacent to what we own? |
| Team | Is there a named, accountable, dedicated owner with a track record? |
| Traction | What evidence beyond the business case: pilots, customer commitments, test results? |
| Product & moat | Does it use advantages the group has that competitors can't buy? |
| Why now | What does waiting cost: share, a competitor's move, a regulatory deadline? |
| Business model | Margin vs group average; cannibalisation of existing revenue; pricing consistency. |
| Capital efficiency | NPV > 0 at the hurdle rate, IRR > hurdle, payback within policy, funding in stage gates. |

Additional corporate red flags:
- Only one option presented (no "do nothing" or alternatives)
- Cash flows not incremental (include revenue the group would earn anyway)
- Sunk costs used to justify further spend
- No kill criteria or stage gates
- Risk to the core business (brand, channel conflict, regulatory exposure) not addressed

## Bootstrapped Review: The Self-Funding Test

For a bootstrapped profile, the first question is not "is this investable?" but "does this need outside money at all?" Check:

1. **Time to founder salary**: months until revenue covers the founders' minimum personal costs.
2. **Cash conversion**: can customers pay upfront (annual, deposits, retainers) to fund growth?
3. **Cheapest next test**: what proves or disproves the next step for under one month of costs?
4. **What outside money would buy**: if capital would only accelerate something already working, it's optional. If the business can't work without it, the model may be wrong for bootstrapping.
5. **Cost of capital**: angel money at this stage typically costs 10–20% of the company; revenue-based financing or customer prepayments may be cheaper.

Verdict options for bootstrapped: **Don't raise** (self-fund) · **Raise a small angel round** (and what it buys) · **Rethink the model**.
